A research project in progress · English fiction, 1800–1929

What Stands in the Way

Every plot needs something between a character and what they want. A storm, a rival, a rule about who may marry whom, a letter that never arrives, a fear the hero cannot master. This project reads thousands of novels to ask a simple question: as the world modernized, did the things standing in the way change?

How the reading works

A machine that reads for plot, checked by people who read for a living

  1. STEP 1

    Build the library

    15,511 English-language novels from Project Gutenberg, de-duplicated, dated, and sorted into genres.

  2. STEP 2

    Sample the pages

    24 random passages of about 350 words from each novel, so no book or author dominates.

  3. STEP 3

    Read for the obstacle

    An AI model running on volunteers’ computers names what is in the way and must quote the exact sentence that shows it.

  4. STEP 4

    Check with human readers

    Students and an English teacher label a random sample blind, so the machine’s mistakes can be measured and corrected.

  5. STEP 5

    Test the history

    Pre-registered statistics ask whether obstacles shifted over time, and whether the answer survives every alternative explanation.

A first look at change · preliminary

Did novels turn inward?

Critics from Erich Kahler to Virginia Woolf describe the modern novel moving inward, from adventures in the world to struggles in the mind. If that is true, the obstacles in plots should shift toward the character’s own doubts and fears.

The first labeled passages do not show that. The share of inner obstacles stays near one in eight and dips slightly after 1885, while obstacles from the material world (weather, distance, darkness, illness) edge up. In earlier pilot labels this rise held within every genre, so it is not just the flood of adventure stories on later shelves.

These are the AI’s labels before human correction, from one half of the library. Bars show 95% ranges from resampling novels. Few novels survive from before 1850, so that era is uncertain. The confirmatory test runs on the sealed second half after students and a teacher check a random sample.

Machines are almost never the obstacle

This project began with the telegraph, the railway, and the penny post. Yet only 38 of 2,531 obstacles name a technology at all. The telegram leads with 10. Even in an age of new machines, novelists kept putting people in the way.

Why counting words is not enough

A word count would score both passages below as “labor” or “letters.” Only one of them puts something in a character’s way. Telling the difference is the skill of a careful reader, and it is what the project measures.

Where you come in

Thirty passages, fifteen minutes

The study’s results are only as good as its checks. An English teacher’s judgment on 30 short passages becomes the expert reference that every student annotator is measured against. The questions are the ones you would ask in class: is something in the way, what kind of thing is it, and does it come from the world, from other people, or from the character’s own mind?
