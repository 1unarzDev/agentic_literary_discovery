# Critical theses and what the hypotheses actually test

Verified 2026-10-31 against publisher/platform descriptions, available previews, and institutional catalogues. These are paraphrases, not claims to have read inaccessible full books. Page-specific evidence was not established; no page references are supplied. **UNVERIFIED** below identifies a proposed inference that should not be attributed to the critic.

**Erich Kahler, *The Inward Turn of Narrative* (1973).** Kahler describes narrative history as an interaction between consciousness and external reality, with a long tendency toward internalization: narrative incorporates more of the external world within an expanding consciousness. His trajectory runs from ancient narrative through the **end of the eighteenth century**, according to Princeton's description, rather than directly establishing a nineteenth-century trend. H1 therefore extends and operationalizes his account by testing whether obstacles become relatively more internal during 1800–1925. Equating greater psychological representation with a higher prevalence of internally located plot obstacles is the project's inference, **UNVERIFIED as Kahler's own proposition**. [Publisher-hosted JSTOR record](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt1m3nwc3).

**Richard Menke, *Telegraphic Realism: Victorian Fiction and Other Information Systems* (2008 in contemporary author/institutional citations; platform catalogue also gives 2007).** Menke places Victorian fiction within the emergence of modern communication media, from postage stamps and electric telegraphy to wireless, arguing that realism responds to the possibilities and problems of information flows. H2a can test one measurable consequence of this media history by distinguishing infrastructural communication breakdowns from intentional concealment. The specific claim that intentional withholding progressively replaces accident across English fiction is **UNVERIFIED as Menke's thesis**; his account supplies motivation, not a verified directional frequency prediction. [Official Stanford-hosted distribution record](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9781503626720/html); [Oxford author biography identifying the book as 2008](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/28178/chapter-abstract/213046732).

**Kate Thomas, *Postal Pleasures: Sex, Scandal, and Victorian Letters* (2012 copyright; Oxford online publication 2011).** Thomas examines how Victorian postal networks, workers, and material objects support queer relations and sexual intrigue. Her account shifts attention from the contents of letters toward their circulation and mediation: postal fiction uses the system to cross boundaries of class, marriage, and sexuality. For H2a this makes secrecy, surveillance, social constraint, and human handling plausible sources of communication obstacles even when delivery infrastructure improves. It does **not** by itself establish a decline in accidental failures or a monotonic rise in withholding; that directional extrapolation is **UNVERIFIED**. [Oxford abstract and metadata](https://academic.oup.com/book/2281); [Oxford copyright record](https://academic.oup.com/book/2281/chapter-abstract/142392890).

**Karin Koehler, *Thomas Hardy and Victorian Communication: Letters, Telegrams and Postal Systems* (2016).** Koehler connects Hardy's letters, telegrams, and notes with nineteenth-century communication infrastructure, material culture, identity, privacy, and social convention, showing how increasingly affordable written communication creates narrative possibilities. This supports treating communication as a plot mechanism rather than counting postal vocabulary. H2a tests whether obstacles arise from delivery conditions or from characters' decisions and social relations; extrapolating Hardy-specific close readings to an aggregate historical trend remains the project's inference, **UNVERIFIED as Koehler's own cross-corpus claim**. [Springer/Palgrave publisher description and contents](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-319-29102-4).

**Wolfgang Schivelbusch, *The Railway Journey: The Industrialization of Time and Space in the Nineteenth Century* (verified 2014 edition).** Schivelbusch argues that rail travel helped produce a learned industrial consciousness, changing perceptions of distance, time, speed, autonomy, landscape, and risk. H2b's travel-obstacle/setting distinction is a useful empirical extension, but railway modernization also generates shock, accidents, isolation, and compartment dramas in the book's account. Accordingly, a simple claim that railways remove narrative obstacles is **UNVERIFIED** and too strong: test changes in the kinds and roles of travel obstacles, and allow new railway-specific obstacles. [UC Press description and contents](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/the-railway-journey/paper).

**Wolfgang Schivelbusch, *Disenchanted Night: The Industrialization of Light in the Nineteenth Century* (verified 1995 UC Press paperback; English 1988 edition separately catalogued).** Schivelbusch links artificial illumination to modern consciousness and social organization, including streetlamp politics, nightlife, shop windows, bourgeois interiors, and theatre. H2c can test whether darkness loses its practical role as a barrier while night acquires other narrative functions. However, falling darkness-obstacle rates in novels are **UNVERIFIED as his stated prediction**; a cultural history of lighting cannot alone establish that literary frequency trend. [UC Press description and contents](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/disenchanted-night/paper); [1988 English-edition catalogue](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL2063344M/Disenchanted_night).

**A. Roger Ekirch, *At Day's Close: Night in Times Past* (2005 original; verified Norton 2006 paperback).** Ekirch reconstructs night as a distinct preindustrial social world, encompassing danger, crime, fire, illness, moonlight, work, leisure, intimacy, sleep, and dreams. This motivates coding darkness as a material condition while keeping nighttime setting separate from obstruction: night already supported positive and social functions before modern lighting. H2c is therefore a test of changing distributions of functions, rather than the emergence of non-obstacle night from nothing. The claim that darkness obstacles decline specifically in fiction during 1800–1925 is **UNVERIFIED as Ekirch's thesis**. [Norton description and edition metadata](https://wwnorton.co.uk/books/9780393329018-at-day-s-close); [2005 original edition library record](https://search.worldcat.org/title/1022659464).

**Additional checks.** Catherine J. Golden's *Posting It* (2009) describes cheap postage as a major expansion in access that also enabled blackmail and fraud: infrastructure can create new obstacles as it removes old ones ([official scholarly platform](https://academic.oup.com/florida-scholarship-online/book/28987)). Ian Carter's *Railways and Culture in Britain* (2001) examines canonical and popular railway representations, including crime and comic fiction; genre composition is therefore a material confound for H2b ([publisher](https://manchesteruniversitypress.co.uk/9780719059667/)). Nicholas Daly's *Literature, Technology, and Modernity, 1860–2000* (2004) treats human–machine encounters and fictional suspense as part of modernization of subjectivity; railway danger may increase narrative urgency despite faster transport ([official front matter](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/33929/frontmatter/9780521833929_frontmatter.pdf), [introduction](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/33929/excerpt/9780521833929_excerpt.pdf)).
